How nationalist and authoritarian
politics shape exhibition design

Context

Master’s thesis and exhibition concept, MA Exhibition Design, FH JOANNEUM Graz, 2026

Exhibition concept

“The power of staging”, designed for the Graz Museum

Supervisor

FH-Prof. Dipl.-Rest. Erika Thümmel

Target audience

Young people aged 14 to 30

Download the full master’s thesis (PDF, German, 30 MB)

Problem
Statement

Museums are places of education, knowledge, participation, historical reckoning, and public memory. The public generally views them as more trustworthy than scientists. At the same time, museums are not neutral. As institutions that tell stories, select objects, and create meanings, museums wield significant cultural and political influence. As such, they can also become the focus of political influence, as is currently happening in the US under Donald Trump or as occurred in Hungary under Viktor Orbán. This influence is not always obvious. Structural mechanisms such as executive measures, funding decisions, institutional restructuring, the replacement of leadership personnel, or the non-renewal of contracts are used to reshape museum narratives, reinforce national identity narratives, or marginalise controversial issues such as colonialism, migration, or minority rights.

These developments cannot be viewed separately. They are linked to a global rise in authoritarian and anti-democratic tendencies, in the wake of which cultural institutions are increasingly becoming part of an ideological culture war. This debate is not limited solely to institutional structures, collections, or exhibition themes. It also concerns the way in which history, identity, and societal realities are staged and conveyed. Exhibition design therefore emerges as a previously underexamined dimension of political influence and a key concern of this study.

Research Question

Are museums today directly influenced by nationalist and authoritarian forces? How does this influence affect them, and what role does exhibition design play?

Theory

The theoretical part of the thesis is built on four central premises:

1. Museums are not neutral

According to curator and theorist Nora Sternfeld, it was long taken for granted that museums produce identity, address the distinction between “the self” and “the other”, reproduce national boundaries, and display valuable objects and objective values. Since the New Museology of the 1970s, this view has been challenged: whose history is told, and whose is left out? Studies from the 1990s show that museums actively create meaning through the topics, objects and narratives they choose (Sternfeld, 2017, p. 190).

2. Museums create truth

In “Power/Knowledge”, Michel Foucault shows that truth is not an absolute fact. It is produced by systems of power and knowledge in society (Foucault, 1980, pp. 131–133). The same applies to museums. By deciding what to show and what to leave out, they claim to tell the truth. Because people trust museums, this claim is rarely questioned.

3. Museums depend on politics

In democracies, governments do not usually intervene in museums directly. However, museums still depend on public funding, which makes them susceptible to political influence. In authoritarian states, the influence is direct: governments replace museum directors, control funding and dictate which narrative an exhibition has to tell.

4. Design shapes how content is understood

How objects and content are presented is an essential part of every exhibition. In his book “Exhibition Design”, David Dernie writes: “Scale, color, materials, lighting, sounds and graphics will affect the way in which the display communicates to the visitor: the reading of the object will change according to its context and presentation” (Dernie, 2006, p. 6). Exhibition designers therefore help decide how an exhibition is understood.

Based on the theory, expert interviews and analyses of exhibitions in Hungary, the US and Austria, I developed a catalogue of content and design features that mark nationalist-authoritarian exhibitions. No single feature proves political influence on its own. It only becomes visible when several of them come together.

Testing

To test whether visitors actually recognise these design features, I created a fictional person, Hans Falkner. Based on the same ambiguous biography, I designed three versions of an exhibition about him. The nationalist-authoritarian version was based on the US Freedom Trucks, the critical version on the House of Austrian History in Vienna, and the ambivalent version on a white-cube aesthetic.

The test was qualitative and took place in the video studio at FH JOANNEUM. Participants walked through all three versions while thinking aloud and then answered a survey. The participants clearly recognised the nationalist version as right-wing and linked its red and blue colours to right-wing populist parties. Sound and imagery had the strongest effect on their perception.

Final
Concept

“The power of staging” brings together the findings from theory, analysis and testing. The exhibition asks how design is used to communicate, strengthen and construct political narratives, and it questions the museum as an institution. Its aim is to make visitors, especially young people, aware of how design and narratives work, so they can recognise political staging and question it.

Narrative Structure

The exhibition is designed for three rooms on the second floor of the Graz Museum. The route is linear, so visitors would pass through the rooms in a fixed order. This follows the narrative structure: the first room is immersive and emotional, while the second and third rooms are factual and analytical and present their content in a more traditional way.

Visual Identity

I deliberately avoided the design features of nationalist-authoritarian exhibitions and chose materials and colours that set a counterpoint to them. The main material is clear polycarbonate. It stands for transparency: the exhibition shows its own design decisions instead of hiding them. Light green serves as the accent colour.

Typography is the one exception. I combined a traditional serif typeface with a sans serif on purpose. The serif stands for nationalist-authoritarian design, the sans serif for a democratic design language. This shows on a typographic level that design can do very different things depending on the political context.

Room 1
Immersive Installation

In the first room, design itself becomes the subject of the exhibition. Visitors experience directly how design and narrative change the way content is perceived. The story is built around the fictional Hans Falkner. The room is white and grey, and all colour comes from projections, so the atmosphere can change without changing the room. The installation begins with a nationalist-authoritarian narrative that presents Falkner as the hero of Austria’s post-war rebuilding. This narrative is then disrupted and questioned, until a critical counter-narrative reveals his Nazi past.

Room 2
Museums under Pressure

The second room explains what visitors have just experienced. Four topics take up the key questions of the thesis: Where does manipulation through design begin? What is shown and what is left out? How does design shape our perception? Who decides what is told? Each question has its own interactive station. One is a sliding “censorship filter” that hides every passage of a museum text that doesn’t fit the national myth. Another lets visitors change the light, sound and label of a pair of old leather boots, which become a soldier’s boots or the boots of a refugee.

Room 3
Nation

The last room deals with the idea of the nation and how it is used politically: flags and anthems, folk culture and sporting events. National identity is not shown as bad in itself, but as ambivalent. It can create belonging and orientation, but it can also exclude others. Questions such as “How is the idea of a nation formed?”, “What meaning do we give national symbols?” and “How is a nation made?” close the exhibition and lead back to the topics of the museum analysis.

Conclusion

Museums are under pressure from a global shift to the right and a rise in autocracy, as the US shows. In Europe, too, it is a growing problem. That is why it matters now to keep designing critical exhibitions that support democracy. My exhibition concept does this by translating nationalist-authoritarian mechanisms into a spatial format. It doesn’t only explain how manipulation works: it lets visitors experience it, so they can question it.

My analysis can only show these mechanisms through examples, not prove them across the board. The study is limited to two case studies in the Western world. A larger and more diverse study could show how these mechanisms appear in other contexts.
